Choose an STL orientation by ranking the requirement that matters most: a critical face, load direction, stable contact, support access or printer fit. Compare a few plausible candidates, then make the final decision from the target slicer's supports and layers. No geometry score is universally best.

Rotation changes bed pose; reflection changes handedness. If asymmetric features remain on the wrong side after rotating, check reflected handedness before choosing an orientation. An intentional left-hand variant is a design decision, not a poorer bed-orientation score.

1. Rank the requirement that cannot be fixed later

Priority Question to answer before rotating
Cosmetic face Which visible face must avoid support marks, seams or bed texture?
Mating feature Which rim, bore or locating face needs the most controlled layer and support condition?
Load path Where will bending, pull or impact act relative to layers and the loaded root?
Stability Which candidate gives intentional bed contact without making the part tall and unstable?
Fit Which rotated envelope fits the usable printer volume and all process allowances?

Material, bonding, perimeters, temperature and geometry all affect mechanical behaviour. Orientation is one input, not a guaranteed strength result.

2. Compare the six axis-aligned candidates

The orientation planner compares +Z, -Z, +X, -X, +Y and -Y from the source model. For each candidate it exposes the rotated X/Y/Z envelope, nominal rectangular printer fit, downward-facing triangle area and a coplanar contact estimate. It does not search arbitrary angles, generate supports, predict warping or infer the most important face. If the immediate problem is an imported tilted base, follow the lay-flat workflow.

3. Follow the critical feature

Illustrative choices, not tested print results
Part feature Candidate worth comparing Trade-off to inspect
Horizontal bore Bore axis vertical or horizontal Layer shape, support contact and final sizing procedure
Enclosure rim Rim away from support and first-layer effects Overall support demand and bed contact elsewhere
Loaded hook root Load path compared with layer direction Support and finish sacrificed to protect the root
Visible plaque face Face upward or deliberately bed-facing Top finish versus bed texture, seam and opposite-side support

4. Separate overhangs, bridges and islands

A downward face angle does not reveal whether the slicer will anchor a bridge, grow an overhang from prior material or start an island above empty space. Use the bridge-versus-overhang comparison at the first affected layer. If a region begins without model below, use the floating-region diagnosis to separate connected geometry from detached components and generated support.

5. Check contact and support removal

A larger coplanar contact estimate can be a useful stability signal, but it is not an adhesion or warping prediction. Separately inspect whether generated support can leave holes, channels and enclosed regions without damaging a critical face. The internal support-access workflow keeps those questions distinct.

6. Verify in the actual slicer

  1. Confirm the rotated envelope against usable printer X/Y/Z, edge allowances, brims, supports and purge structures.
  2. Inspect the first layer, bed-contact region and any required adhesion feature.
  3. Step through support and interface paths, including their removal direction.
  4. Inspect the first bridge layer, its anchors and bridge direction.
  5. Review seams and support contact on visible and mating surfaces.
  6. Compare layer direction with the actual load path and test a representative feature when failure matters.

7. Record the chosen compromise

Save the slicer project and profile with the final rotation. Add a short note naming the protected feature and the accepted compromise, such as extra support to protect a loaded root or greater height to keep a sealing face clean. That record is more reproducible than a universal “best orientation” claim.
