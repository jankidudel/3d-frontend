Calculate starting hole and pin dimensions

Measure holes and pins separately

An undersized hole and an oversized pin can produce the same failed assembly. Measure both features in the orientation in which they were printed. Use clean, cooled parts and an appropriate gauge, pin or caliper technique; forcing caliper jaws into a small rough hole can produce a misleading value. Take several measurements and record the direction because X/Y and vertical features can behave differently.

Work a compensation example

Suppose a CAD hole is 10.00 mm and three controlled prints measure 9.68, 9.72 and 9.70 mm. Their average is 9.70 mm, an observed diameter shortfall of 0.30 mm. A provisional CAD test hole of 10.30 mm is reasonable for the same printer, material, orientation and profile. This is not a universal offset: print a coupon with nearby sizes, then select the smallest hole that provides the required fit without excessive play.

Clearance is not compensation

Compensation addresses a repeatable difference between designed and produced size. Clearance is the intentional gap between mating parts. Establish machine behaviour first, then choose clearance for sliding, locating, press-fit or fastened parts. Do not add the same number blindly to every radius, pin and slot.

Validate before changing the production model

  1. Use the intended nozzle, material, layer height, orientation and profile.
  2. Print a compact coupon containing the relevant hole and pin range.
  3. Measure after cooling and test the real mating component where possible.
  4. Update a copy of the CAD model and inspect the exported dimensions.
  5. Repeat after hardware, material or orientation changes.

If roundness is poor or values vary around the circumference, investigate belts, motion, cooling, extrusion and seam placement before applying a scalar offset.

Horizontal and vertical holes behave differently

A vertical-axis hole is built as repeated perimeter loops; a horizontal-axis hole includes overhangs and may sag or facet differently. Seam position, elephant-foot effects near the bed and support contact can also make one region of a hole smaller than another. Record orientation with every measurement and avoid transferring one correction between orientations without a test.

Prefer model-level evidence over one measurement

Test several nominal sizes around the real feature and include the intended pin, bearing or fastener. A consistent offset across sizes supports compensation; a changing or irregular error suggests geometry, motion, extrusion or cooling needs attention. Measure pins as well, since reducing a pin may preserve more wall around a hole in some designs.

For holes near the first layer, check for bed compression before editing the entire bore. For safety-critical or load-bearing assemblies, follow the hardware and material design guidance and validate the finished joint physically.

Separate a hole from a clearance decision

A printed hole that measures undersize and two parts that bind are related but different problems. Measure the printed feature with suitable calipers, compare it with the CAD dimension, and test the intended pin or fastener rather than assuming a nominal diameter is enough. For example, if a 10.00 mm designed hole repeatedly measures 9.70 mm, the observed difference is 0.30 mm; whether to change the hole, the mating pin, or the assembly clearance depends on the fit you need. Validate a changed design with a small coupon before changing an important part.

Use the tolerance calculator to document the selected clearance. Compensation is profile- and orientation- specific, and it does not replace calibration, correct extrusion, or a slicer preview.

A hole designed at 10 mm does not always print as a clean 10 mm hole. Extrusion width, curved perimeters, cooling, shrinkage and feature orientation can all reduce a printed diameter. Scaling the whole model is usually not the answer.

Start with the fit you need

Loose clearance, sliding motion, a snug lid and a press fit need different gaps. Decide whether the hole passes a bolt, guides a pin or grips a part permanently, then model dimensions for that intent. The Tolerance & Clearance Calculator provides a starting CAD dimension from your own test print.

Measure a simple test

Print several holes and pins near the sizes you use, with the final printer, material, nozzle, layer height and orientation. Measure with calipers and note whether holes are undersized, pins oversized or both. Consistent whole-model error belongs in the Shrinkage Calculator; feature-specific error belongs in clearance or hole compensation.

Check surrounding geometry

Thin walls around a hole can deform or slice poorly. Inspect them with the STL or 3MF Wall Thickness Checker, then verify line count and orientation in the slicer.

Practical cautions

  • Drilling or reaming may be better for precision holes.
  • Horizontal and vertical holes can need different compensation.
  • Flow calibration makes extrusion more consistent but does not remove the need for clearance.
  • Record successful values by material and profile.

Plan a test fit before altering your CAD model

Keep compensation local and reversible

Apply the tested change to a named model revision or parametric feature rather than silently rescaling the whole part. Note the printer, profile, axis and coupon result in the design. When sending the model elsewhere, provide the uncompensated source or explain the baked-in correction; another machine may need a different value. Re-check wall thickness around enlarged holes and edge distance around fasteners before export.
