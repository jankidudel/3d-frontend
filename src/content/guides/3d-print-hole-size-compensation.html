Calculate starting hole and pin dimensions

Compare CAD, exported mesh and produced readings first

Keep the intended CAD diameter, a named construction on the serialized/reopened mesh, and the produced-part reading as three separate records. In the original 12-sided bore control, a circle through separated boundary vertices gives about 20 coordinate units, while the ideal flat span is only 19.3185165258. That apparent nominal circle is not evidence that a 20-unit pin fits, or that any printed compensation is appropriate. See how to interpret a constructed diameter before attributing a mismatch to the printer. Physical readings and a controlled compensation coupon still determine the next process adjustment.

Measure holes and pins separately

An undersized hole and an oversized pin can produce the same failed assembly. Measure both features in the orientation in which they were printed. Use clean, cooled parts and an appropriate gauge, pin or caliper technique; forcing caliper jaws into a small rough hole can produce a misleading value. Take several measurements and record the direction because X/Y and vertical features can behave differently.

Choose the hardware target before correcting it

A clearance passage, thread-forming pilot and insert seat need different targets even with the same screw label. Choose screw role and passage sizing first. In a hypothetical fixed-hardware example, a 3.95 mm passing envelope plus 0.35 mm chosen diametral clearance gives a 4.30 mm produced target. With assumed comparable hole error eH = -0.15 mm, the provisional source test is 4.30 - (-0.15) = 4.45 mm. The chosen gap is separate from local error; these are not universal hardware settings.

For a bearing seat, the separately manufactured outer ring supplies the fixed OD; correct the printed housing against that selected input. In a printed pin/bore pair, both errors matter: a 4.40 mm bore with -0.12 mm error and a 4.00 mm pin with +0.08 mm error leave 4.28 - 4.08 = 0.20 mm diametral gap. Those are illustrative assumptions, not print results. Neither local circular correction sets axial allowance; no axial measurement was supplied.

Work a compensation example

In an illustrative example, a CAD hole is 10.00 mm and three controlled prints measure 9.68, 9.72 and 9.70 mm. Their average is 9.70 mm, an observed diameter shortfall of 0.30 mm. A provisional CAD test hole of 10.30 mm is reasonable for the same printer, material, orientation and profile. This is not a universal offset: print a coupon with nearby sizes, then select the smallest hole that provides the required fit without excessive play.

Clearance is not compensation

Compensation addresses a repeatable difference between designed and produced size. Clearance is the intentional gap between mating parts. Establish machine behaviour first, then choose clearance for sliding, locating, press-fit or fastened parts. Do not add the same number blindly to every radius, pin and slot.

For a split-print socket and dowel, compare the produced socket diameter with the produced pin diameter after applying the same sign convention. In the illustrative alignment-pin example, a designed 4.30 mm socket and 4.00 mm pin have 0.30 mm nominal diametral clearance, while hypothetical readings of 4.18 mm and 4.08 mm leave only 0.10 mm. The selected clearance and the two production errors remain separate inputs.

Choose CAD edit, slicer offset or whole-part scale

A parametric CAD edit changes the selected source feature and is visible in the exported geometry. A slicer offset changes the documented contours during toolpath generation and may not appear in an unchanged exported STL. A whole-part scale changes outer spans, hole diameters, centre spacing and walls together. Use whole-part scale only when representative axis measurements support that different task.

For a slicer control, check current official documentation for your exact version, sign, contour scope and radius-versus-diameter convention. This guide deliberately gives no numerical slicer-offset conversion. Record the source revision and active profile so a baked CAD correction is not applied again in the slicer. Keep eH = mean produced hole - designed hole, then model hole = target produced hole - eH in the worksheet. The empirical offset does not transfer automatically across sizes or orientations.

Validate a local, reversible change

  1. Use comparable sizes, the intended hardware, material, orientation and profile. Keep raw direction-labelled readings; irregular roundness needs diagnosis before an offset.
  2. Test a compact representative feature, including the local boss/wall and intended post-processing. Measure pins as well as holes; a mean alone does not bound variation.
  3. Edit a named source-CAD copy and record the correction. Reopen the exact export, check surrounding walls/edge distances and inspect the actual sliced paths.
  4. Retest after hardware, material or orientation changes. When handing off a compensated model, explain the baked-in correction so it is not applied twice.

Drilling or reaming may be a separate planned finishing operation. It needs its own access, remaining-material and final-dimension checks; changing a scalar offset does not establish those.

Localise the restriction before correcting size

Record bore-axis orientation and the height/direction of each reading. Use the orientation diagnosis for directional restrictions and the base-flare guide for bed-facing interference. Their procedures should precede a scalar hole offset.

Check surrounding geometry

Enlarging a hole consumes local material. Use the STL or 3MF Wall Thickness Checker for a bounded screen, then inspect actual local paths and validate the finished joint against its physical requirements. Flow consistency does not remove the need to choose clearance.
