Hollowing removes material from inside a model while keeping an outer shell. It is common for large resin prints and can also be useful for some FDM models.

Source and further reading

Formlabs' official hollowing documentation explains its software's topology behavior and the need to plan drainage for relevant resin workflows. Treat its process guidance as product-specific and check the documentation and safety information for the exact printer and material you use.

Hollowing depends on the process

For resin printing, hollowing can reduce material but introduces drainage, venting, washing and curing decisions; trapped liquid is a design and handling risk. For FDM, infill and perimeter settings often provide the relevant material control without changing the mesh. Place openings where they can drain in the chosen orientation, preserve adequate structural walls, and inspect cross- sections before exporting. Re-check the modified file for watertightness and use the slicer preview as the final process-specific validation.

FDM and resin need different decisions

In FDM, slicer perimeters and infill usually control internal material without editing the outer mesh. A manually hollowed STL can create unsupported internal ceilings that are difficult to inspect or remove supports from. For resin, hollowing can reduce material and peel forces, but creates a cavity that must be drained, washed, vented and cured according to the resin and printer workflow.

Plan drain and vent paths with orientation

A drain opening should allow liquid to leave at the intended print and wash orientations; a vent can prevent a sealed cup from trapping pressure or suction. The number, diameter and placement are application-specific. Follow printer, resin and slicer manufacturer guidance rather than copying a universal hole size. Avoid placing openings where supports, hardware interfaces or visible surfaces make them unusable.

Look for trapped volumes

Move cross-sections through the complete model and inspect separate cavities, narrow passages and low points. A visible opening into one chamber does not drain another disconnected chamber. Internal lattices or accidental surfaces can also block flow even when the outer shell appears correct.

Validate the hollowed copy

  1. Preserve the solid original and save the hollowed model separately.
  2. Check wall thickness and transitions around openings.
  3. Inspect cross-sections for sealed pockets and internal intersections.
  4. Re-check watertightness/manifoldness where a closed shell is intended.
  5. Review internal supports, drain paths and every layer in the slicer.

Hollowing changes structure and post-processing risk. InspectMesh can help inspect geometry locally, but it cannot select safe resin handling, curing or mechanical wall requirements for a particular job.

Do not hide an uninspectable interior

Before export, keep a sectioned screenshot or source model showing wall and opening placement. After mesh conversion, confirm internal faces have not intersected the exterior and that openings remain connected to every intended cavity. If the interior cannot be inspected confidently, a smaller solid print or redesigned source model is safer evidence.

The goal is to save material and reduce weight without making the shell too weak to print or use.

Start with a clean model

Before hollowing, check the STL with the STL Quality Checker. Hollowing works best when the model is a closed, consistent solid.

Use the STL Watertight Checker to look for open edges. A model with holes or non-manifold geometry can confuse the process because the software may not be able to tell what is inside the object.

If the file has simple repairable defects, try the STL Repair Tool first, then validate it again.

Choose a suitable shell thickness

The right shell thickness depends on the printing method and the model’s purpose.

For FDM printing, walls need to work with your nozzle width, perimeter count and infill strategy. In many cases, configuring perimeters and infill in the slicer is more practical than hollowing the mesh itself.

For resin printing, hollowing is often more important because a fully solid large model can use substantial resin and create high peel forces. The shell still needs enough thickness to survive handling and curing.

Add drain holes for resin prints

A hollow resin model needs a way for uncured resin to escape. Add drain holes in low, hidden areas that will be reachable during washing and curing.

Without drainage, resin can remain trapped inside the model. This can create a messy print, make cleaning difficult and cause later problems as trapped resin expands or leaks.

Plan the print orientation before placing holes. The lowest points during printing are often the most useful drainage locations.

Check the result before printing

After hollowing, inspect the model in your slicer and review the layer preview. Confirm that the shell remains thick enough, holes are in sensible positions and supports do not block drainage.

Use the STL Dimensions Checker if you need to confirm the overall size after editing. Hollowing should not change the outside dimensions, but checking avoids accidental scaling mistakes.

Hollowing is a design and print-preparation decision, not simply a file-size trick. A clean mesh, suitable wall thickness and a proper drainage plan matter more than removing the maximum possible material.

Balance savings against risk

Estimate the material benefit in the slicer before accepting the added interior and post-processing complexity. A small part may save little, while a large sealed cavity can create substantial cleanup risk. Follow the resin safety data and equipment instructions for handling uncured material; geometry guidance does not replace protective equipment, ventilation, washing, curing or disposal requirements.
