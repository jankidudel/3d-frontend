Match the recess to the head's bearing surface. A counterbore creates a cylindrical recess with a flat seat; a countersink creates a conical seat. Check the head drawing, passage and remaining material together before choosing either shape. A flush appearance alone does not establish a suitable bearing seat.

Choose the surface and access result you actually need.
Arrangement Geometry to establish Access question
Head remains above the surface Compatible bearing face, passage and washer if used Does the protruding head obstruct the adjoining part or service tool?
Flat-seated counterbore Recess diameter, axial depth, flat seat and local material Can the head, washer and driver enter the cylindrical recess?
Conical countersink Included angle, opening and throat planes, actual head profile Can the driver approach with the head at its intended seating position?

Read the exact head drawing

Thread size does not determine one universal head diameter or angle. Identify the exact head style, under-head transition, drive envelope, washer and dimensional tolerances. For a specific example, Bossard BN 2100 lists a 90-degree countersunk family with ISO 10642 and approximate DIN 7991 / UNI 5933 relationships (reviewed 1 November 2026). That identifies this family; it neither makes all metric or imperial countersinks 90 degrees nor establishes interchangeability between similarly named standards. Obtain the selected variant drawing.

Flat seat: separate axial and radial material

In a hypothetical 5.0 mm-thick plate, a counterbore 2.0 mm deep leaves 5.0 - 2.0 = 3.0 mm axial material below its flat seat around the through-hole. Within a concentric circular boss of diameter 14.0 mm, a 7.2 mm recess leaves an ideal radial difference of (14.0 - 7.2) / 2 = 3.4 mm.

These describe geometry, not required floor/wall values or strength. An eccentric recess, nearby edge, boss root or other local shape changes the material. Keep the through-hole diameter separate from the larger head recess: passage follows the screw's role and passing envelope, while recess dimensions follow the head and assembly access.

Conical seat: use the included angle

Define D at the opening plane on the top surface and d at the lower plane where the ideal cone meets the cylindrical passage. Their axial separation z for an ideal conical frustum is z = (D - d) / (2 tan(alpha / 2)). With hypothetical D = 7.2 mm, d = 4.5 mm and included angle alpha = 90 degrees: z = (7.2 - 4.5) / (2 tan(45 degrees)) = 1.35 mm.

The included angle is 90 degrees; the half-angle is 45 degrees. This equation describes the ideal recess, not verified contact with a manufactured screw. Under-head transitions, tolerances and a rounded or protruding head can change the seating result. A small entrance chamfer is not automatically a matching countersink. Compare the entire relevant head profile with the intended recess in CAD rather than judging contact from a shaded viewport.

Recheck reach when the bearing position moves

A deeper recess moves the bearing seat and changes screw projection into the receiver. Include washers and every intervening plate in the stack. Head styles can use different length-reference conventions, so nominal screw length alone cannot establish reach. Follow the projection example and retain tip, runout and usable engagement as separate requirements. If the receiver is a nut, check its pocket and replacement route as well as the head-side recess.

Check the whole assembly approach

  1. Place the exact head and any washer at the intended bearing position in the source design.
  2. Check the bit or socket envelope and approach direction, including nearby ribs and covers.
  3. Check the screw insertion route and whether another component blocks it before tightening.
  4. Plan how the driver and hardware can be removed for service, not just the final seated pose.

The head can fit while the tool cannot reach it. A head-side recess is also distinct from an insert installation seat or counterbore in the receiver. Do not borrow a headed insert's outer dimension for a screw-head pocket.

Inspect saved geometry and the actual sliced region

Open the exact saved copy in the STL Viewer or 3MF Viewer. Source CAD owns the analytic cone angle, diameters and bearing planes. Viewer distance/angle picks describe selected mesh points: a valid three-point angle can be a chord construction rather than the intended head-seat profile. Use the measurement workflow to retain the selection basis and unit/frame.

Inspect above and below the recess shoulder with visual clipping, recording expected void and material. Clipping does not measure contact or extract a closed section. Then review the chosen bore orientation and support-removal access in your actual slicing workflow. Retain the final source revision, profile and unresolved seat/access checks. No new slicer run or physical assembly result is claimed here; representative physical seating and service checks remain necessary for acceptance.
