A sensible 3D-print price begins with cost, but it does not stop there. A customer price needs to cover the plastic, printer time, your work, the risk of failures and any fees or delivery that are included in the offer.

Allocate failure risk without double counting

Choose one explicit method. You can multiply production cost by an observed retry factor, add a fixed expected-failure allowance, or use a minimum charge for jobs that consume setup time. Do not add all three without checking what each covers. If nine out of ten comparable runs succeed, dividing the consumed production cost by 0.9 is one transparent starting point; it is not evidence for a new material or untested geometry.

Separate hands-on labor from machine time. Ten unattended print hours do not equal ten labor hours, while twenty minutes of file repair, support removal and packaging should not disappear inside an electricity estimate. Record assumed minutes and hourly rate so repeat orders can reuse setup knowledge without charging for work that was not repeated.

Check the quote after the order changes

Quantity, color changes, rush delivery, customer-supplied files and tolerance requirements can change the risk and work. Re-run the quote instead of scaling the old total mechanically. A ten-part batch may share preparation but place more value at risk on one build plate. State revision and cancellation terms before starting any paid design work.

Worked quote: margin is not markup

Assume calculated production cost is $18, hands-on preparation and finishing adds $12, an expected-failure allowance adds $3, and packaging adds $2. The pre-fee cost is $35. If the selling channel takes 10% of revenue and the target profit margin is 25% of revenue, solve price as $35 / (1 - 0.10 - 0.25) = $53.85. Rounding the quote to $54 leaves roughly $13.50 profit after the $5.40 fee and $35 cost.

Adding 25% to $35 would produce $43.75, which is a 25% markup on cost, not a 25% margin on revenue. Once the 10% fee is removed, that price leaves only $4.38 profit, or about a 10% margin. State which method the quote uses so later updates do not silently mix the two.

Define the quotation boundary

A useful quote identifies quantity, material and color, quality or layer-height assumptions, included design work, finishing, delivery, tax treatment, lead time, and how revisions are handled. Separate a one-time setup or modeling fee from the per-part price when that makes repeat orders clearer. For uncertain jobs, say what triggers a revised quote rather than hiding the uncertainty in an unexplained premium.

Recalculate when quantity or process changes: batching can reduce setup labor, but a full plate can increase the value of a single failed run. The calculator supplies a transparent floor and scenario model; demand, rights to manufacture, service level and local competition still require business judgment.

Quote from calculated cost, not intuition

Start with a documented production cost, then add payment fees, customer communication, finishing, risk and the intended margin. Margin is profit as a share of selling price; markup is the increase over cost. State what the quote includes, when it expires and what design changes trigger a revision.

The 3D Printing Price Calculator keeps those pieces visible, so a quote is easier to explain and you can see what profit remains after selling fees.

Start with direct production cost

Slice the part using the printer, material and settings you expect to use. Copy the slicer’s filament weight and print time. These are more useful than a model-volume estimate because they reflect infill, walls, supports and orientation.

Then add:

  • material, based on the spool price and grams used;
  • electricity, based on printer power, run time and your tariff;
  • machine wear or maintenance allowance;
  • labour for setup, support removal, finishing and checking.

Read How to Calculate 3D Printing Cost for the underlying material and energy calculation, and use How Much Filament Does a 3D Print Use if you need to interpret the slicer figure.

Add costs that are easy to miss

Add magnets, screws, paint and other parts separately. Include packaging and shipping only when they are included in the quoted price. A failure allowance is useful for reprints and waste; apply it to the whole pre-failure cost, not only the filament.

If the part is untested or geometrically risky, check it before committing to a price. The STL Quality Checker and 3MF Quality Checker can identify common mesh issues, though neither guarantees a successful print.

Account for selling fees before setting margin

Marketplace and payment processors can charge a percentage, a fixed fee, or both. A price that looks profitable before fees can be a loss after them. Enter those fees before choosing a target margin.

Margin means profit divided by selling price. Markup means profit divided by cost. For example, a £10 cost sold for £15 has £5 profit: 50% markup but about 33% margin. Keep the terms separate when comparing prices.

Create a transparent quote

  1. State what the printed part includes.
  2. Say whether shipping is included.
  3. Show sales tax separately when it applies.
  4. Describe the figure as an estimate if material use, delivery or fees may change.

The calculator’s downloadable quote follows this structure. It is useful for clear communication, but it is not an invoice, tax filing tool or accounting system.

A calculator cannot set market value

Cost and target profit help you avoid undercharging, but they cannot tell you whether a customer will accept a price. Design value, local competition, turnaround time, finishing quality and delivery expectations still matter. Compare your quote with the market and revise it deliberately rather than treating a suggested price as a guarantee.

Price a batch with one order setup cost

Illustrative order: five identical parts cost 4.00 each to produce, and the order needs one 10.00 setup and packing charge. Total cost is 5 × 4 + 10 = 30.00; allocated cost per item is 6.00. A 30% gross margin needs a quote of 30 / (1 - 0.30) = 42.86 for the order, or about 8.57 per part. A 30% markup instead gives 39.00 and a lower margin. These are different decisions.

Do not repeat the entire fixed setup charge for every item unless setup is actually repeated. Conversely, five separate print runs can require repeated unloading and inspection labour. Write down whether your input is per item, per plate or per order before multiplying it by quantity. Compare one plate with several plates using real sliced time and usage.

Build a customer-price calculation with explicit currency and quantity, and keep a dated copy of the assumptions with the quote. A browser-local report is not invoicing, tax calculation, customer management or a cloud quote history. Shipping, marketplace fees and taxes need their own actual inputs where relevant.
