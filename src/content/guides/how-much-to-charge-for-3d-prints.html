A sensible 3D-print price begins with cost, but it does not stop there. A customer price needs to cover the plastic, printer time, your work, the risk of failures and any fees or delivery that are included in the offer.

The 3D Print Price Calculator keeps those pieces visible, so a quote is easier to explain and you can see what profit remains after selling fees.

Start with direct production cost

Slice the part using the printer, material and settings you expect to use. Copy the slicer’s filament weight and print time. These are more useful than a model-volume estimate because they reflect infill, walls, supports and orientation.

Then add:

  • material, based on the spool price and grams used;
  • electricity, based on printer power, run time and your tariff;
  • machine wear or maintenance allowance;
  • labour for setup, support removal, finishing and checking.

Read How to Calculate 3D Printing Cost for the underlying material and energy calculation, and use How Much Filament Does a 3D Print Use if you need to interpret the slicer figure.

Add costs that are easy to miss

Add magnets, screws, paint and other parts separately. Include packaging and shipping only when they are included in the quoted price. A failure allowance is useful for reprints and waste; apply it to the whole pre-failure cost, not only the filament.

If the part is untested or geometrically risky, check it before committing to a price. The STL Quality Checker and 3MF Quality Checker can identify common mesh issues, though neither guarantees a successful print.

Account for selling fees before setting margin

Marketplace and payment processors can charge a percentage, a fixed fee, or both. A price that looks profitable before fees can be a loss after them. Enter those fees before choosing a target margin.

Margin means profit divided by selling price. Markup means profit divided by cost. For example, a £10 cost sold for £15 has £5 profit: 50% markup but about 33% margin. Keep the terms separate when comparing prices.

Create a transparent quote

  1. State what the printed part includes.
  2. Say whether shipping is included.
  3. Show sales tax separately when it applies.
  4. Describe the figure as an estimate if material use, delivery or fees may change.

The calculator’s downloadable quote follows this structure. It is useful for clear communication, but it is not an invoice, tax filing tool or accounting system.

A calculator cannot set market value

Cost and target profit help you avoid undercharging, but they cannot tell you whether a customer will accept a price. Design value, local competition, turnaround time, finishing quality and delivery expectations still matter. Compare your quote with the market and revise it deliberately rather than treating a suggested price as a guarantee.
