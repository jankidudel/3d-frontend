Before printing a 3D model, you may need to know how big a specific feature actually is. A bounding box gives the model's full width, height and depth, but it cannot tell you the distance between two holes, the width of an opening or the length of a tab.

Measure holes and curved features carefully

Selecting two visible mesh vertices on a faceted circle does not necessarily measure its nominal diameter or center. The export may not place vertices at opposite extrema, and coarse tessellation can make the result depend on viewing angle. Fit or inspect the underlying analytic feature in CAD when diameter, concentricity or center distance controls assembly.

For an approximate mesh check, take several spans, inspect a cross-section and compare with a known reference. State whether the value is a bounding extent, vertex distance, estimated diameter or source-CAD dimension. Those labels make apparently conflicting measurements easier to reconcile.

Separate intent, exported geometry and the physical part

Source CAD owns nominal analytic diameter, centre spacing and datums. The tessellated export owns faceted surfaces and resolved transforms. Physical measurement owns the produced dimension under the recorded instrument/method and conditioning. A file span cannot establish a printed bore diameter, and a physical reading cannot identify whether its cause is CAD, export or process without comparison.

After checking the file, record comparable produced dimensions and exclusions in the fit worksheet or axis worksheet. Use direction- and height-labelled sets for bores; do not click two arbitrary points and report them as fitted analytic centres.

Keep transformation state visible

Measure the resolved object that will be printed. A slicer may show a transformed instance while another viewer reports the unscaled resource. Note any scale or rotation applied after import, reset accidental transforms before comparing applications, and export a test copy only when the receiving workflow requires baked geometry.

Worked measurement sequence

Suppose a replacement cover must fit within a 120 × 80 × 25 mm envelope and align two holes 64 mm apart. First read the axis-aligned bounding box and test sensible rotations; a result of 118 × 78 × 28 mm fails height even though two axes fit. Confirm the 64 mm analytic hole-centre spacing in source CAD; surface picks in the mesh viewer cannot establish those centres. These are different questions: the bounding box cannot confirm the hole spacing, and two selected points cannot prove the overall envelope.

Record the assumed unit beside every number. If an STL intended as inches opens as millimeters, a 4-inch span may appear as 4 mm; applying 25.4 gives 101.6 mm. For 3MF, verify the declared unit and resolved transform rather than applying a guessed factor. Preserve more decimal places during calculation than the manufacturing process can reproduce, then round only the communicated result.

Mesh vertices approximate the exported surface, so a picked point may not be a true CAD center, tangent or nominal diameter. Use the original CAD model for tolerance-critical inspection. Use mesh measurement to screen size, placement and obvious discrepancies, and validate final fit with the actual printer and process assumptions.

Choose the measurement type

A bounding box answers overall fit; point-to-point measurement answers a feature size. Confirm units before comparing either value with a drawing or printer volume. For example, a 120 x 80 x 40 mm box may fit a printer while a critical 8 mm hole still needs a separate measurement and tolerance check.

Use the STL Viewer or 3MF Viewer to open a model locally, inspect it in 3D and measure the distance between two visible points.

Start with overall dimensions

Check the complete size first. Overall dimensions are useful for confirming printer fit and spotting an incorrect scale before you inspect individual features.

If a model is 250 mm wide and your usable build area is 220 mm wide, the fit issue is clear without feature measurement.

Use point-to-point measurement for specific features

Point-to-point measurement helps with a slot width, mounting tab, lip, flange or protruding feature. Analytic hole-centre spacing belongs in source CAD, because selecting two mesh surface points does not locate those centres. In the Viewer, activate Measure distance, then select two visible points on the mesh.

The result shows the direct distance and the difference along the X, Y and Z axes. Direct distance helps with diagonal features; axis differences show how far the feature changes in each direction.

Understand the limits of mesh measurement

An STL or 3MF viewer measures the mesh you opened, not the original parametric CAD design. Curves are represented by triangles, so a low-resolution source can approximate the intended design. Your selected points also affect the result.

Use viewer measurement as a practical inspection tool. For a tight mechanical fit, return to the CAD model where possible, or print and measure a calibration test using the intended printer, material and settings. For tolerance planning, use the 3D Print Tolerance & Clearance Calculator.

STL units need extra care

STL usually stores triangle coordinates without reliable information about whether values mean millimetres, inches, centimetres or another unit. A measurement can be mathematically correct but physically interpreted incorrectly. When using the STL Viewer, choose the intended unit before treating a result as a real-world dimension, then confirm model scale with the STL Dimensions Checker.

3MF can provide more unit context

3MF files can declare a package unit, which makes scale interpretation more reliable when the package is valid and supported. The 3MF Viewer shows its resolved model and available unit information. Confirm the resulting physical dimensions after conversion or when receiving files from an unfamiliar workflow.

Measure, then verify

Measuring one feature does not replace a complete pre-print check. Inspect the model, check overall dimensions and printer fit, measure important visible features, check mesh integrity and wall thickness where relevant, then run STL Print Preflight or 3MF Print Preflight on the final file before slicing.

A measured model is easier to trust

Point-to-point measurement does not turn a mesh viewer into a CAD package. Combined with overall dimensions, unit checking and Preflight, it provides a practical way to understand the file you received before material and print time are committed.

Use four measurements for four different decisions

Measurement What it answers
Global axis-aligned bounds How far the current orientation spans X, Y and Z; not a named feature size
Two selected mesh points Direct distance between those surface locations and their axis differences
CAD dimension The intended analytic diameter, constraint or centre spacing
Physical measurement The produced part under the measuring method and conditions used

A rotated 20 mm cube can have a larger axis-aligned span without changing its edge length. A clicked point on a polygonal hole is on a facet, not automatically its analytic centre. Repeat selections and inspect the camera angle before interpreting a small difference as a design change.

Tessellation deviation is another source of uncertainty: a mesh approximates curved CAD surfaces. Stop using the viewer as the acceptance authority when the tolerance depends on an exact cylinder or datum. Use the source dimension and a separate physical inspection plan.
