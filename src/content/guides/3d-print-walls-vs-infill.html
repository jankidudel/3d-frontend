Walls follow the model's boundaries, solid top and bottom regions close its surfaces, and sparse infill occupies the remaining internal region. Changing one does not automatically replace the others. Choose the affected feature first, then inspect its generated paths and complete material estimate.

Map the region before changing a count

Three process regions, with different questions
Region What to inspect What its setting cannot prove
Side and perimeter boundaries Paths around outer faces, bosses, openings and thin ribs A count alone does not establish actual material thickness, retained detail or strength
Solid top and bottom caps Closure layers, effective depth, local overrides and the paths beneath a roof A nonzero top count does not establish a supported or correctly generated roof
Sparse interior Pattern, density, anchors and local internal support The density setting is not a percentage of the complete part mass

A slicer wall/perimeter count differs from a material wall already modelled in CAD. A narrow modelled wall may leave little or no sparse interior at all. Neither term means a disconnected mesh shell; if the question is connectivity, use the separate-parts explanation.

Increasing infill does not necessarily restore an omitted rib, repair a source hole or add a missing top surface. For a thin material region, keep the measurement and generated-path comparison with wall-thickness interpretation. For absent roof paths, follow the visibility, source and effective-settings diagnosis.

Width and overlap make counts approximate

Perimeter count multiplied by one chosen width is equal-width arithmetic, not exact slicer wall thickness. Actual widths can vary and adjoining lines can overlap. Prusa's layers and perimeters reference describes these concepts and separate top/bottom settings. Use the nozzle and wall planner for its bounded arithmetic, then inspect the native paths. A sampled STL thickness result measures uploaded material geometry; it cannot see future infill or certify a chosen perimeter count.

An original shell, cap and core material budget

This is a simplified geometry model, not a slicer prediction or tested print. Start with a hypothetical solid 30 × 30 × 20 mm rectangular prism. Assign uniform side-shell thickness t and solid top and bottom thickness of 1.00 mm each. The remaining ideal rectangular core contains an assumed uniform material fraction p.

  • V_outer = 30 × 30 × 20 = 18,000 mm³
  • V_core = (30 - 2t) × (30 - 2t) × (20 - 2)
  • V_shell_and_caps = V_outer - V_core
  • V_material_model = V_shell_and_caps + p × V_core

Here p=0.20 is an assumed core material fraction. It does not assert that any slicer's 20% setting creates exactly this volume. Patterns, overlap, widths, extra solid regions and local changes can differ. Subtract the core once to count shell corners and caps once; independently adding six face volumes would overlap regions.

Divide mm³ by 1,000 to obtain cm³, then multiply by the illustrative density. Full-precision masses are 8.71801344 g and 10.249344 g; the table rounds only for display. The density is an input, not a universal material specification. These thicknesses are not recommended settings, and their mass difference proves no strength difference. An irregular shape, openings or variable thickness needs a different construction; this is not a universal infill estimator.

Why multiplying solid mass by infill fails

The naive blanket calculation is 18 cm³ × 1.24 g/cm³ × 0.20 = 4.464 g. It omits the distinct shell/cap allocation in both cases. Even this toy model shows that 20% internal material does not mean 20% of solid part mass.

The original solid mesh stays at 18 cm³ in both hypothetical process scenarios, with a solid-material equivalent of 22.32 g at that density. The STL volume tool and 3MF volume tool cannot detect these assigned process regions or output these two analytical print estimates. Creating a real CAD cavity changes geometry and its exported volume; that distinct operation belongs to the hollowing guide.

Inspect caps and what supports them

A solid top region and its underlying internal paths are related but separate. Prusa's infill reference describes internal support for top layers as well as density and anchoring controls. Inspect the first closure paths, their anchors and their spans. A roof over sparse internal paths is not explained by the infill percentage alone; use bridge and overhang interpretation for the actual path situation.

Zero sparse infill does not itself remove perimeters or disable top/bottom closure. It also does not automatically select continuous spiral-vase mode. A roof can still require a supportable process path. At the other end, nominal 100% infill does not establish zero porosity, complete process fidelity or structural suitability.

Prusa's pattern reference distinguishes internal patterns and surface-fill choices. Pattern labels do not establish the strongest option for every load or a mechanical result for this part.

Separate structural requirements from settings

For a load-bearing part, identify the load path and direction, geometry, material/process, orientation and joints. Compare representative physical tests for the relevant requirement. A wall or infill change cannot certify strength; vendor descriptions of settings do not establish a universally best combination. No physical testing was performed for this guide.

Compare complete generated jobs

  1. Retain the original project, revision, profile and requirement: preserve a thin feature, close a roof, change mass or investigate a load path.
  2. Change one setting group deliberately and retain all effective local overrides. A named profile can change several settings.
  3. Inspect the affected layers and required features in the exact output.
  4. Compare complete per-material consumption and supplied duration. Do not replace them with the box model or a blanket infill multiplier.
  5. Use the reviewed new totals for production cost and conduct any required physical trial before accepting the revised part.
