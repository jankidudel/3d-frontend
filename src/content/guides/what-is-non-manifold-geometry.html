A mesh is manifold when every edge is shared by exactly two triangles, like the seams of a closed box. Non-manifold geometry breaks that rule: an edge belongs to only one triangle (a hole) or to three or more (extra faces meeting at one edge). Slicers warn about it because they cannot tell what is inside and what is outside at that spot.

The three edge cases

Triangles on one edge What it means What you still have to decide
One Open edge: the surface has a hole or gap here Whether the opening was meant to be there
Two Normal: the edge is properly closed Nothing about edges; faces could still be flipped
Three or more Non-manifold edge: extra faces meet here Which face is the extra one

Problems an edge check cannot see

Some slicing problems look like non-manifold errors but are something else. You can download each example and try it yourself:

Example file What the checker reports The real question
Open cube 10 faces, 4 open edges Was one side meant to be missing?
Closed cube 12 faces, no edge problems Is it the right shape?
Duplicate face 13 faces, 1 duplicate, 3 non-manifold edges Can the copied face simply be removed?
Separate cubes 24 faces, 2 pieces, no edge problems Are two parts intended?
Two parts touching at one point May count as one piece; no edge problem Is a real joint intended? Check the CAD model.
Crossing cubes 24 faces, 2 pieces, no edge problems (overlap not tested) Should the two solids be merged?

Surfaces that pass through each other share no edge, so an edge count never sees them. That is a separate check, explained in self-intersecting mesh vs non-manifold. The CGAL mesh-processing manual treats the two as separate tests too.

How InspectMesh counts it

The STL Manifold Checker compares exact corner positions, skips zero-area triangles when counting edges, and groups pieces that share any corner. It does not detect every non-manifold corner (vertex) case or decide which parts you meant to keep. "No open edges" means no holes were found, not that the model is ready to print.
